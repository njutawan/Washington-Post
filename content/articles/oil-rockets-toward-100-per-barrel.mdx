For the second time in two years, American motorists are confronting the prospect
of $100-a-barrel oil — and the sticker shock that comes with it.

## Why now?

A confluence of OPEC+ production cuts, resilient summer travel demand and a
sharper-than-expected slowdown in U.S. inventory draws has lifted Brent crude
roughly 25 percent since June.

> "We haven't seen demand destruction yet because the labor market is so strong,"
> one industry analyst said. "That makes the shock harder to absorb."

For the White House, rising fuel prices represent an unwelcome October surprise.
Administration officials have in recent weeks privately pressured Saudi Arabia
to reverse course, according to two people familiar with the discussions.

## What it means for consumers

Analysts say pump prices typically lag crude by two to three weeks, meaning
drivers could see $4-a-gallon regular before Thanksgiving in some markets.

- Diesel prices have already climbed 12 percent this quarter
- Jet fuel costs are pressuring airfares
- Shipping companies have added fuel surcharges
